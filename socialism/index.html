What is socialism?

Socialism, framed as the "collective ownership of the means of production," takes property and freedom away from individuals by force in the name of "the people."

This can be done in entirety, with brutal confiscations, or through a softer way: redistributive taxation, mass state employment, excessive regulations, price, wage, and rent controls. In either form, socialism consists of direct or indirect stealing, limiting people's freedom and making them dependent on the state.

There's an abundance of socialist measures, but they all share the same pattern: politicians promise larger groups that they will legalize stealing from smaller groups and share the loot as "free" services, handouts, or subsidies. In exchange, they ask for power.

The problem with this deal is not only that it's immoral, but that in the long run it hurts the overall economy and makes most people poorer and more oppressed—including those who at first appear to benefit.

Socialists promise free things. How they finance those things is simply with other people's earnings. They frame their policies as "equality," "solidarity," or "social justice" to legitimize their votes-for-stolen-money proposition.

Once understood, it's easy to see how their "solutions" pave the way to poverty and totalitarianism: power concentrates in the state, real opposition becomes impossible, and democracy is dismantled in practice.

Socialism's track record

Wherever it was tried on a full scale, socialism led to poverty, oppression, and genocide. Few in the West know about the Gulag, the Pitești experiment, the Cambodian Killing Fields, or the Great Leap Forward.

The killings, the famines, the torture, the persecutions—they all happened during attempts to build a fairer society, by forcefully subordinating personal interests to the "common good."

The death toll of communist regimes, which implemented socialism, is estimated at more than 100 million victims—about 20 times the number of Jews killed in the Holocaust.

Large parts of the world lived in dystopian prison states for generations, where people lived like slaves and could never challenge their ruthless rulers—democracy dismantled, opposition impossible. North Korea and Venezuela are present-day reminders of such horrendous worlds.

The centrally planned economies of socialist states always end up generating extreme poverty, shortages and famines. Where socialists didn't take full control, there is generally more freedom and prosperity—but socialist measures still inhibit growth and cause victims, albeit in less visible ways.

How socialism leads to totalitarianism

Since socialism implies economic control, it requires the centralization of power and restricting people's freedom to offer services and to produce, buy, and sell freely at the price they agree on.

By limiting rights to run businesses, trade, own property and accumulate capital, socialists transfer power from private individuals to the state—and to the politicians and bureaucrats who run it. Once this power transfer surpasses a certain level, instituting a dictatorship becomes a mere formality.

As state control over the economy increases, regime critics lose their voice. It becomes harder to accumulate the resources needed for an effective opposition: organizations, academia, the media, they all become complicit, and the ability to meaningfully challenge those who run the state is lost.

Citizens have to accept whatever is imposed by those who claim to represent the majority—including limitations on free speech and freedom of assembly. There's simply no one left who can effectively stand up and say no.

Once power is centralized, real opposition becomes impossible—and democracy is de facto dismantled. Individual freedom dies, and liberal democracy can never exist except for a brief time in the beginning. At best, the tyranny of the majority prevails. More often, the arbitrary will of the one-party leaders becomes the law of the land.

How socialism leads to poverty

When someone proposes simple solutions to persistent problems, remember there is no such thing as a free lunch. When judging a policy, all consequences have to be considered, not just the first, most obvious one.

"Free" education and "free" healthcare sound great. But who pays? How is a state-run system held accountable? How can quality increase if competition is suppressed?

Confiscating profits through taxation looks positive at first: recipients do better for a while. But companies have less left for growth, research, and to reward investors.

Governments then raise tariffs, give subsidies, or nationalize companies—protecting producers from competition and removing incentives to improve. Costs increase, quality decreases, and consumers pay the price: poor products, shortages, waiting times, and generalized economic decline.

Imposing a minimum wage is another "magical" solution. Companies forced to pay more avoid hiring or go bankrupt; they automate or outsource; prices rise. The worst effect is that it can make it harder for young, inexperienced workers to find jobs where they learn skills.

One of the biggest fallacies is that national wealth has a fixed size. In reality it changes constantly—and stealing from the rich often makes the poor even poorer in the long run.

Democratic socialism: why it's a delusion

Some admit the disasters of the past, but say they support "democratic socialism," which they claim is different. It is not. All socialist regimes have claimed to be democratic, to have the support of "the people."

Many added it to their country's names: the German Democratic Republic, Democratic Kampuchea, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea. Obviously, there was nothing democratic about these countries.

People are lured with the promise of a better life and deceived that they are entitled to others' property. Once supporters reach critical mass, state-backed stealing is normalized.

A massive plundering ensues, centralizing power in the bureaucrats who control the process, restricting economic freedom, and paving the way to totalitarianism. Stealing private capital dries out initiative and innovation and makes people poorer and more dependent on the state.

Once that power is centralized, real opposition becomes impossible—elections may remain as theater, but democracy is dismantled in all but name. By the time people realize they need to change those in power, they often no longer have the means to do so.

An ideology of envy and hate

Some claim socialism is the moral course of action. "From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs" is a nice-sounding slogan. But socialism is based on coercion and stealing, which are not moral actions in any altruistic belief system.

In practice, its long-term results never correspond with its stated goals. Average citizens in socialist countries always end up poorer than those in free, capitalist countries. The road to hell is paved with good intentions.

The first step is to confiscate earnings and property from those perceived to have "too much." The benefit to the poor is short-lived. Less private capital means less wealth creation. A weaker economy hurts the most vulnerable the most.

What is confiscated often doesn't even reach the poor, because of bureaucracy, waste, and corruption.

The moral course of action is true compassion and charity—voluntary help, sacrificing our own time and resources. Being generous with other people's money is not generosity; it's hypocrisy. Voting for those who say "the rich have to pay their fair share" is a proof of bitterness and envy, not altruism.

Societal degradation under socialism

Besides paving the path to economic collapse, socialism does something much more destructive: it inverts society's values, by punishing integrity and success and rewarding incompetence and failure.

Socialists first target the most successful people—often murdering them or sending them to work camps. Even without violence, they replace competent individuals unwilling to join the stealing scheme with unskilled, obedient activists.

The most prestigious positions end up occupied by the most immoral and incompetent. Meritocracy is inverted. This counter-selection hurts institutions and distorts values for generations—and leaves no independent base from which real opposition could form.

When integrity is systematically punished while stealing and blind obedience are rewarded, societal decay and generalized suffering are inevitable. Once power and every major institution answer to the same center, democracy cannot survive: opposition is only permitted when it is harmless.

Socialism also fosters the mindset that the state is responsible for our destiny, prosperity and well-being. A nation of doers can transform into a nation of passive complainers who expect the state to provide education, jobs, a "living wage," housing, even entertainment—well beyond the state's original purpose: security and the rule of law so free people can live and prosper.

The Nordics

The Scandinavian countries are routinely given as counterexamples for the failures of socialism, despite the fact that none of them is actually socialist. They consistently rank as some of the most business-friendly countries in the world.

Their corporate income tax rates are often more competitive than in the United States, while Denmark and Sweden don't even have a state-imposed minimum wage.

It's true they can afford generous welfare programs, but factors that have nothing to do with socialist policies contribute: small populations, rich natural resources, small defense expenditures, a pro-business environment, and functioning rule of law.

The truth about the Nordic model is distorted to advance the socialist cause. These countries are indeed rich, but that's not because of socialist measures but in spite of them.

Nazism, atheism, and socialism

While Nazism is the classic example of a dangerous ideology, socialism is presented as the kind and compassionate alternative. The strong ties between them are shamelessly covered up.

Nazism stands for national-socialism. Hitler's party shared many values and policies with socialist platforms still promoted today: greater state control of the economy, prices and wages, anti-capitalism, attacks on Christianity, and the sacrifice of individual freedom in the name of the "collective interest."

"We are on the side of labor and against finance." That sounds familiar—but those are the words of Joseph Goebbels.

The Nazis' antisemitism was also rooted in anti-capitalism: "As socialists we are opponents of the Jews because we see in the Hebrews the incarnation of capitalism." It is this hatred of economic freedom, individual liberty, and envy of success that fueled both Nazism and communism—two sides of the same collectivist mentality.

It is no coincidence that most socialists are fanatical atheists. Wherever they got to power, they marginalized, persecuted, and often killed clergymen.

"Enlightened" intellectuals reject God so they can place themselves in His position and impose "paradise on Earth." Without a fixed moral system, abuse, persecution, stealing, and even murder become necessary tools for a noble cause. The Party and the State become the supreme authority. The end result is invariably a living hell—yet they keep insisting the next time will end differently.

Real progress and how to reduce poverty

Those who truly care about the poor and the oppressed should support individual freedom (including economic freedom—so capitalism), the rule of law, and a moral culture that condemns theft and envy.

Economic liberalization in China, India, the Eastern Bloc and elsewhere is responsible for the spectacular 80% decrease in extreme poverty in recent decades. It is the biggest gain in living standards in history, achieved by simply lessening the state grip on the economy, leaving entrepreneurs to flourish and generate wealth. That's the real progress.

Who knows how many lives could have been saved if those countries hadn't implemented socialism in the first place? Christians advocate voluntary generosity; socialists advocate "forced generosity"—being generous with other people's money, which is not generosity at all.

Freedom is like good health—you don't realise how important it is until you lose it, and once lost, it can be impossible to get back.

Once power is centralized and real opposition is gone, democracy is already dismantled, even if the ballots still look familiar. And freedom is too often taken for granted in the West.
